Retirement Planning: Where to Start

7 min read Retirement

Retirement can feel too far away to plan for, especially early in your career. But retirement planning isn't really about picking a number decades from now — it's about building habits today that quietly compound in your favor.

Why starting early matters more than starting big

Because investment growth compounds, money contributed in your 20s and 30s has far more time to grow than the same amount contributed in your 40s and 50s. Someone who contributes smaller amounts starting early can end up ahead of someone who contributes more but starts a decade later — the head start does a lot of the work.

The three main pieces of Canadian retirement income

  • Government benefits: Canada Pension Plan (CPP) and Old Age Security (OAS) provide a base level of income, based on your contributions and residency history.
  • Workplace savings: Your group investment account (such as a Group RRSP) through Life Ready, plus any employer matching contributions.
  • Personal savings: Additional accounts like a TFSA or personal RRSP that you contribute to outside of work.

Most people rely on a combination of all three — government benefits alone are rarely enough to fully replace pre-retirement income.

Setting a realistic goal

A commonly used rule of thumb is aiming to replace roughly 70% of your pre-retirement income, though your actual number depends on your expected expenses, mortgage status, and lifestyle plans. The goal doesn't need to be exact today — it needs to be good enough to guide a contribution rate you can start with.

A simple way to think about your contribution rate

  1. Contribute at least enough to capture any employer match in full
  2. Increase your contribution rate gradually — for example, with every raise
  3. Revisit your rate and fund choices once a year, not just once and forget it

It's never too early — or too late — to start

Whether you're just starting your career or thinking about retirement in the next several years, a short conversation about your specific numbers is usually more useful than a general rule of thumb.
